## Abstract

China emits about one-third of the world's fossil carbon dioxide; its transport fuels add about 1.8 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent a year, well to wheel. We built an open annual least-cost model of a petroleum-free transport system for mainland China, including the five autonomous regions, Hong Kong and Macau, and for Taiwan. It links electric vehicles and expressways, reactor hubs whose heat cascades from hydrogen production to fuel upgrading, drying and food production, and liquid fuels from residues, bamboo, hemp, regional crops and seaweed. It tests annual resource adequacy, not hourly operation. In the central 2050 configuration the system costs 303.4 billion United States dollars per year, 24.0% less than the same services supplied with petroleum at late-2025 pre-tax prices, and cuts well-to-wheel emissions by 94.8%. This is an optimistic point: across 1,000 draws from the assumed ranges the median saving is only 8%, and the system is cheaper in 65% of draws. The saving rests mainly on battery-electric cars costing no more to own than petrol cars: without that advantage it falls to 14.7%, if they cost 100 dollars a year more to 4.7%, and with the least favourable ownership-cost derivation the system costs 36% more than petroleum. A combination of moderately adverse assumptions makes the system 12% dearer than petroleum at late-2025 prices, though still cheaper at November 2026 prices. Most hydrogen comes from steam electrolysis at the reactor hubs; methane pyrolysis, limited by markets for its carbon, would use 33 billion cubic metres of natural gas a year, and without gas the central saving is 17.4%. All five autonomous regions supply more renewable liquid fuel than they use, while Taiwan's island system costs more than petroleum. A related United States model, corrected for a heavy-truck unit error, saves 12% rather than 23%.

**Keywords:** China; transport decarbonization; high-temperature gas-cooled reactor; steam electrolysis; biofuels; electric vehicles; robustness analysis

**Teaser text:** This study models a petroleum-free transport system for China, its autonomous regions, Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan. Electrification, reactor heat and regional biofuels could cost about one-quarter less than petroleum if electric cars stay no more expensive to own than petrol cars; vehicle costs, carbon markets, biomass supply and build rates set the limits.

## 1 Introduction

China is the largest emitter of carbon dioxide. The Global Carbon Project attributes about 32% of the 38.1 Gt of fossil CO~2~ projected for 2025 to China [1]. Its transport sector is a smaller share of national emissions than in the United States, about 0.9 to 1.1 Gt CO~2~ at the tailpipe [2, 3], but it is large in absolute terms and, unlike power, is still dominated by oil. In 2025 China consumed about 155 Mt of gasoline, 203 Mt of diesel and 40 Mt of jet fuel [4], and used around 43 billion cubic metres (bcm) of natural gas in trucks and buses [5]. Including fuel production and refining, we estimate the well-to-wheel emissions of the transport services in our scope at 1,804 Mt CO~2~e in 2025.

China is also further along in transport electrification than any other large economy. New-energy vehicles were 49% of new car registrations in 2025, and the stock reached 44 million [6]. Zero-emission trucks were a quarter of medium and heavy truck sales [7], and gasoline and diesel demand has started to fall [8]. What remains is harder: aviation, shipping, off-road machinery and the long tail of heavy freight still need dense fuels or very large electricity and hydrogen supplies. Studies that treat one mode or one fuel at a time can miss the shared limits on biomass, hydrogen, heat and electricity [9, 10].

A related open model of United States transport [11], currently under review, uses high-temperature reactor hubs to supply heat and hydrogen, electric roads and vehicles to carry most road travel, and land and ocean biomass to supply liquids. Here we build the equivalent model for China with Chinese data and technologies: the pebble-bed high-temperature gas-cooled reactor now operating at Shidaowan [12], the thorium molten-salt reactor programme at Wuwei [13], the sodium fast reactor at Xiapu [14], light-water small modular reactors [15], the world's largest offshore wind fleet [16] and seaweed industry [17], and the energy crops of the autonomous regions: cassava and sugarcane in Guangxi, sweet sorghum and Jerusalem artichoke in Inner Mongolia, sweet sorghum and cotton stalks in Xinjiang, sweet sorghum and sweet potato in Ningxia, and grassland and forestry residues in Xizang (Tibet).

We ask five questions. First, can a least-cost petroleum-free system serve all 2050 transport services in mainland China, Hong Kong and Macau for less than petroleum, and does it match the 23% saving reported for the United States? Second, how much natural gas would methane pyrolysis consume, and what does hydrogen cost once gas is no longer used? Third, which reactor technology suits the hubs, and is start-up fissile material, including plutonium that might be released by arms reductions, a constraint? Fourth, how do the autonomous regions, the two special administrative regions and Taiwan differ? Fifth, how robust is the result to vehicle ownership costs, carbon co-product markets, residue supply and limits on how fast reactors and grids can be built? We also report the food that reactor heat could grow and how by-products of one farmed species can feed another, keeping these outside the transport cost. All results are conditional screening estimates, not forecasts.

## 2 Materials and methods

### 2.1 Scope and study area

The model covers on-road vehicles, aviation, rail, domestic and bunkered marine fuel, farm machinery, construction and mining machinery, industrial trucks and pipeline compression. Mainland China, including the five autonomous regions, is modelled together with Hong Kong and Macau, which draw electricity and refined fuels from Guangdong. Taiwan has its own grid, refineries and fuel market and is modelled as a separate island system. The 2025 baseline (table S1) uses fuel consumption from the China Association of Petroleum Enterprises' 2025 review [4], transport gas from industry estimates [5], bunker sales [18], Hong Kong jet-fuel imports [19] and, for Taiwan, United States Energy Information Administration data [20]. Diesel is split between on-road, rail, marine, farm, construction and mining, and non-transport uses by assumption; the excluded non-transport share is 14.5 Mt. Baseline transport energy is 19.7 EJ. Services grow by 15% to 2050 in the central case (range 0 to 40%).

### 2.2 End uses in 2050

Technology shares by segment are fixed for 2050 (table S2). Cars are 95% battery-electric, 3% extended-range electric, 1% fuel-cell and 1% liquid-fuelled. Heavy and medium road freight is 35% on overhead-catenary expressways, 45% battery-electric (including battery swapping), 8% extended-range, 8% fuel-cell and 4% liquid. Rail is 90% electric, and aviation uses 97% drop-in jet fuel and 3% hydrogen. Electric and fuel-cell energy use is computed from the displaced fuel energy with drivetrain ratios. For heavy trucks we use 2.40 kWh per mile for battery-electric and 21.1 MJ per mile for diesel, a ratio of 0.41 kWh per kWh of diesel. The United States model divided the same two numbers without converting MJ to kWh. This gave a ratio of 0.11 and understated heavy-truck electricity and hydrogen by a factor of 3.6 (section 3.8 and supplementary section S15).

Vehicle costs enter as the net annual cost of each electric or fuel-cell vehicle relative to a combustion equivalent, excluding energy, which the model costs separately. For cars this term is derived from four components (table S2b): the purchase-price difference annualised over a 12-year life at the discount rate, expected battery replacement, maintenance and repair, and insurance. In August 2025 the average new-energy car sold in China cost about 90% as much as the average petrol car [21], and price parity for mid-range battery-electric cars was projected for 2026 to 2029 [22]. Average transaction prices mix vehicle sizes, segments, features and price competition, so they are not a like-for-like comparison and do not show a durable 2050 advantage; we therefore assume a smaller central difference, with battery-electric cars USD 1,000 cheaper than comparable petrol cars in 2050, and test a high case in which they are USD 500 dearer. Maintenance is taken as 35% lower than for petrol cars, less than the roughly 40% reported per mile [23], because repair costs for new-energy vehicles in China are high. Insurance is the least favourable term. The average new-energy car premium was 4,360 yuan (about USD 610) in 2025 [24], well above the petrol-car average, although much of the gap reflects younger drivers, ride-hailing use and newer, dearer cars; like-for-like premiums are about 20% higher [25]. We use USD 100 per year in the central case and USD 300, about half of today's average premium, in the high case. The resulting central value is a net saving of USD 93 per car per year, with a range from a saving of USD 390 to an extra cost of USD 504. Vehicle stocks are specified for 2050 and are not scaled again by service growth. With 420 million cars in 2050, every USD 100 per car per year changes system cost by about USD 40 billion.

### 2.3 Optimization model

The model is an annual linear programme solved with HiGHS through SciPy [26, 27]. It minimizes the 2050 annual cost

$$C = \sum_i c_i x_i + F,$$

where $x_i$ are activity levels (generation, hydrogen production, biomass purchases, conversion routes, carbon sales), $c_i$ their unit costs and $F$ the costs fixed by the end-use mix (grids, charging, electric expressways, vehicles). Constraints balance electricity, hydrogen, liquid fuel, jet fuel and three tiers of reactor heat; bound biomass tiers, crops, land and renewable capacity; and limit solar to 40% of supply with at least 15% firm supply (table S3; supplementary section S3). The model balances energy annually and has no hourly dispatch.

Electricity comes from onshore wind in two cost tiers (USD 30 and 45 per MWh, plus a USD 20 per MWh firming charge), solar photovoltaics (USD 36 per MWh plus 1.25 times the firming charge), concentrated solar power with storage (USD 95 per MWh), light-water reactors (USD 65 per MWh), offshore wind and reactor hubs. Renewable costs follow the International Renewable Energy Agency's 2025 cost report and Chinese grid research [28, 29]. Hydrogen comes from methane pyrolysis, alkaline electrolysis, steam electrolysis at the hubs (in both eras) and, for 950°C reactors, the sulfur–iodine cycle. Steam electrolysis uses 36.8 kWh of electricity per kg of hydrogen for the stack and balance of plant [30]. Its heat inputs come from a temperature–enthalpy balance per kilogram of hydrogen (supplementary section S8, table S16, fig. S7) rather than from assumption. The stack runs near the thermoneutral voltage at 800°C and 0.5 MPa, with 80% steam utilisation, so 11.2 kg of steam is fed per kg of hydrogen. A high-temperature recuperator, cooled by the hydrogen–steam and oxygen streams leaving the stack, superheats the steam from 152°C to 775°C with a minimum approach of 25 K; the residual sensible heat and condensation of unconverted steam preheat the feed water. What remains is 6.87 kWh of external heat per kg, needed at no more than 152°C for evaporation, and 0.68 kWh per kg of electric topping for the last 25 K and stack heat loss. This is consistent with the observation that, with recuperation, any source of dry steam can be raised to cell temperature with little electrical topping heat [30]. The external heat comes from the reactor's process-steam tier, which in the HTR-PM delivers steam at up to about 500°C [31], or from electric boilers at 98% efficiency, never from heat pumps; topping uses electric heaters unless the reactor outlet is at least 850°C. Recuperators (1.1 m² per kg per hour of capacity, about USD 0.03 per kg of hydrogen) are added to the electrolyser capital charge. All costs are in 2026 US dollars at a 5% discount rate (range 4 to 8%).

### 2.4 Reactor hubs and technology alternatives

The reference hub has six 250 MWt pebble-bed modules feeding one turbine island, the layout planned for the HTR-PM600 [12]. Its heat is split in a cascade: 30% at the 750°C top tier for methane preheat, 7.5% as process steam for biocrude upgrading and steam raising for electrolysis, 10% for drying, the rest to a turbine whose low-grade heat goes to greenhouses and recirculating aquaculture. Module cost follows Wright's law [32],

$$k(n) = k_{\mathrm{FOAK}}\, n^{\log_2(1-\lambda)},$$

with a first-of-a-kind cost $k_{\mathrm{FOAK}}$ of USD 5,250 per kWe and a learning rate $\lambda$ of 8% in the central case (table S4a). Because fleet cost is not convex in fleet size, every published case is solved for every whole number of hubs from 0 to 1,500 and the cheapest is kept; Monte Carlo draws use a 25-hub grid refined to single hubs.

We tested five hub technologies (table S4b): the HTR-PM high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (750°C), a 950°C very-high-temperature reactor, a thorium molten-salt reactor of the TMSR-LF1 lineage (700°C) [13], a sodium-cooled fast reactor of the CFR-600 lineage (550°C) [14] and the ACP100 light-water small modular reactor (about 320°C) [15]. Outlet temperature sets how much of the 8.3 kWh per kg of pyrolysis heat the reactor can supply as methane preheat; the rest is supplied electrically. Turbine efficiency, capital cost and fuel-cycle cost differ by type and are assumptions.

### 2.5 Biomass, regional crops and fuel routes

Crop straw is limited to the collectable surplus implied by agronomic removal limits of 19 to 45% [33, 34]. China produces about 860 Mt of straw a year [34], and the model can use 272 Mt in tiered regional bins at USD 38 to 65 per dry tonne. Forestry and existing bamboo-stand residues add 100 Mt (table S5). New clumping bamboo is limited to 3.0 Mha of hill land in the south and southwest; China already has about 7 Mha of bamboo forest [35]. Hemp is limited to 2.0 Mha in Heilongjiang, Jilin, eastern Inner Mongolia, Yunnan, Shanxi and Gansu (table S6c). The straw bins are net of the straw already used for feed, mushroom substrate, bedding and existing power and pellet plants, and the removal limits are those intended to protect soil organic carbon and against erosion; nutrients removed with straw are replaced with purchased fertiliser at cost. The model does not represent the loss of soil carbon that residue removal can still cause, its effect on soil structure and moisture, or the size of collection radii, so we test residue supply 25% and 50% below the central caps (section 3.2) and add soil-carbon losses as a life-cycle boundary sensitivity.

The autonomous regions contribute specific crops (table S6a,b). Guangxi grows more than 60% of China's cassava and about 60% of its cane sugar [36, 37], and supplies cassava ethanol, molasses ethanol, bagasse and jatropha oil from hill slopes. Inner Mongolia and Xinjiang supply sweet sorghum on saline and alkaline land, Inner Mongolia also Jerusalem artichoke and maize stover, and Xinjiang cotton stalks and non-edible oilseed residues. Ningxia supplies sweet sorghum and sweet potato from the Loess Plateau, and Xizang supplies only grassland and forestry residues. Yak-dung biogas enters the gas balance rather than the liquid fuels. Used cooking oil (5 Mt per year) and woody oil crops supply hydroprocessed fuels.

Residues, bamboo, hemp stalks and sorghum bagasse are converted by hydrothermal liquefaction with hydrotreating, or by gasification and Fischer–Tropsch synthesis with added hydrogen. Starch and sugar ethanol becomes jet fuel through alcohol-to-jet. Conversion yields, hydrogen use, heat demand and jet-fuel fractions are those of the United States model, with Chinese capital costs [11].

### 2.6 Offshore wind and seaweed

Eight mainland zones and one Taiwan Strait zone pair offshore wind with species already farmed in China (table S7): Saccharina japonica off Liaoning and Shandong, Pyropia and Gracilaria rotations off Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Fujian, Gracilaria and Ulva off Guangdong and Guangxi (the Beibu Gulf), and tropical red seaweeds off Hainan. China grew about 60% of the world's farmed seaweed in 2022 [17]. Seaweed between turbines is harvested by solar-powered Grid Harvesters and hauled by battery and wing-sail vessels, using the U.S. model's engineering screen [11]; harvesting at this scale has not been tested.

### 2.7 Petroleum comparator and emissions

The petroleum comparator prices the same 2050 services with the 2025 fuel mix at pre-tax prices. Retail gasoline and diesel caps set by the National Development and Reform Commission are converted to pre-tax prices by removing 13% value-added tax and the consumption tax of 1.52 yuan per litre on gasoline and 1.20 yuan per litre on diesel [38, 39]. In late 2025, 92-octane gasoline sold for about 6.85 yuan per litre. On 13 November 2026 it was 8.41 yuan per litre, after the 2026 Middle East conflict raised oil prices [40] (table S8). Late-2025 prices are the reference; November 2026 prices are 1.37 times higher. The break-even multiple is $m^* = C/P$, where $P$ is the petroleum cost at late-2025 prices; the saving is $1 - m^*$.

Emissions use well-to-wheel intensities for petroleum fuels and road gas, life-cycle intensities for each biofuel route and 1.5% upstream methane leakage for pyrolysis gas [41]. Stored pyrolysis carbon, biochar and livestock methane reductions are not counted against transport emissions.

### 2.8 Natural gas, fissile material, regions and food

A separate gas balance (supplementary section S9) tracks China's demand against domestic production from remaining technically recoverable conventional, tight, shale and coalbed-methane gas [42], pipeline imports rising to the land-import capacity targeted for 2030 and beyond [43], and liquefied natural gas imports up to a ceiling. Reactor fuel is accounted for at inventory level (section S10). We compare the heavy metal needed with China's uranium and thorium resources [44, 45]. Start-up fissile material is compared with plutonium in China's spent fuel [46, 47] and with the International Panel on Fissile Materials' estimate of China's military plutonium [48]. The regional screen splits national demand by fixed regional shares and reports where the least-cost feedstocks come from. The food module allocates low-grade reactor heat to greenhouses, recirculating fish and shrimp farms and Asparagopsis tanks [49]. A cross-feed balance routes fish processing by-products to shrimp feed, shrimp by-products to fish feed, effluent nitrogen to Ulva and kelp to abalone and sea cucumbers (section S12).

### 2.9 Conditional uncertainty analysis

We drew 1,000 triangular parameter sets for each of three cases: least cost, 230 GW of offshore wind with seaweed, and after gas (table S10). The draws cover technology costs, the discount rate, reactor cost case, seaweed yields, biomass and ethanol prices, carbon-market size and value, vehicle costs and service growth. Parameters are drawn independently, and the ranges are modelling assumptions. The share of draws in which the system is cheaper than petroleum is therefore a conditional sensitivity result, not a probability that China's transport costs will fall. Structural uncertainties, such as build rates, adoption, grid reinforcement and policy, are not sampled.

### 2.10 Stress tests and feasibility screens

Because independent draws understate the chance that several unfavourable outcomes occur together, we also solve deterministic stress cases (Table 2): car ownership costs with no electric advantage, USD 100 per year more, and the high derivation; a car stock of 350 or 500 million; no carbon co-product revenue (carbon goes to paid permanent storage), or commodity carbon markets only; residue supply 25% or 50% lower; at most 60 or 30 reactor hubs; transmission and firming costs 60 to 75% higher; and two combinations. The moderate combination joins no electric-car advantage, commodity carbon markets only, 25% less residue, high reactor costs and the tighter grid; the severe combination uses the high car-cost derivation and no carbon revenue instead. A build-rate screen compares the reactor modules and wind capacity that the solutions require each year with current Chinese construction. Life-cycle emissions are recomputed with wider boundaries: 25 g CO~2~e per kWh for all electricity, 3% upstream methane leakage, soil-carbon losses of 20 or 40 g CO~2~e per MJ of residue-derived fuel and 3 g CO~2~e per kWh of hub heat for reactor construction and fuel supply. A stylised wind-lull screen asks whether the inflexible transport load (direct electricity use at 115% of its annual average) can be carried through a windless, overcast winter week when all flexible loads stop and reactor heat goes entirely to the turbines, with wind at 8%, solar at 8% and concentrated solar at 30% of capacity; any shortfall is priced as hydrogen-fired backup capacity (USD 750 per kW) burning stored hydrogen for two such weeks a year. These screens test annual resource adequacy and one stylised stress period. They are not an hourly dispatch, reserve or stability analysis, and they do not establish operational feasibility.

### 2.11 Use of artificial intelligence

Artificial Inventor/Synthetic Scientist V63 assisted with writing model code, collecting and checking input data, running analyses, preparing figures and drafting text. The authors reviewed all outputs and take responsibility for the content.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 The central least-cost system

The least-cost 2050 system costs USD 303.4 billion per year (Fig. 3A). The same services cost USD 399.3 billion with petroleum at late-2025 pre-tax prices and USD 546.0 billion at November 2026 prices. The saving against late-2025 prices is 24.0%, a break-even multiple of 0.76. Petroleum would have to fall 24% below late-2025 prices to match the system.

Final energy falls from 5,469 TWh in 2025 to 3,060 TWh in 2050 despite 15% more service (Fig. 1A). Electricity is 1,443 TWh and hydrogen for vehicles, ships and aircraft is 6.2 Mt; renewable liquids are 1,387 TWh, of which 615 TWh is jet fuel. Onshore wind supplies 931 TWh from 322 GW. In all, 239 reactor hubs (358 GW of heat) supply 1,053 TWh of electricity and the heat for 10.7 Mt of hydrogen from steam electrolysis and 5.8 Mt from methane pyrolysis, for biocrude upgrading and for drying. The optimizer builds no new solar, concentrated solar or offshore wind in this case: at the assumed costs, firmed solar (USD 61 per MWh) is dearer than the hubs and the best wind sites. Solar enters when reactor costs are high (section 3.5) or nuclear power is excluded.

Electricity, grids, charging, electric expressways and rail electrification cost USD 114 billion. Reactor hubs, hydrogen plants, natural gas and hydrogen delivery cost USD 93 billion. Land biofuels cost USD 97 billion, and the net extra cost of electric and fuel-cell vehicles and machines is USD 23 billion. Carbon from pyrolysis (17 Mt per year) earns USD 24 billion in capped markets for vehicle structures, battery anode graphite, tyre carbon black, specialty carbon and metallurgy. These values assume that pyrolysis carbon can be purified, graphitised or otherwise qualified for each use; section 3.2 shows the cost without them. As in the United States, the saving comes mostly from the efficiency of electric drivetrains. Liquid fuels are expensive: the marginal liquid costs USD 210 per MWh, more than three times the pre-tax price of diesel, and every land-biomass tier in the model is used.

Well-to-wheel emissions fall from 2,074 Mt CO~2~e for petroleum in 2050 to 109 Mt, a reduction of 94.8% (Fig. 1B). Liquid fuels account for 80 Mt of what remains. Pyrolysis keeps carbon equivalent to 63 Mt CO~2~ in solid form, which we do not count as a further reduction. Little of it would stay out of the atmosphere for long: 6.3 Mt of carbon goes to products that last one product life, 7.0 Mt to tyre carbon black that is lost as wear particles or burned, and 4.0 Mt to electrodes and recarburiser that are oxidised in use. Only carbon placed in asphalt, concrete or permanent storage, none in this case, would count as durable storage. A logistic transition starting in 2026 avoids about 29 Gt CO~2~e by 2050 (fig. S2). For scale, the 2050 petroleum baseline is about 17% of China's current fossil CO~2~ emissions. Widening the life-cycle boundary raises residual emissions (table S12): to 137 Mt with 25 g CO~2~e per kWh of electricity, 120 Mt with 3% methane leakage, 185 and 261 Mt with soil-carbon losses of 20 and 40 g per MJ of residue fuel, and 309 Mt with all of these and reactor construction, a reduction of 85%. Soil carbon is the largest of these uncertainties; some studies estimate larger near-term losses from residue removal [50].

### 3.2 Conditional uncertainty and stress tests

Across 1,000 draws from the assumed ranges, the least-cost system had a median cost of USD 382 billion (5th to 95th percentile 256 to 543) and was cheaper than late-2025 petroleum in 65% of draws (Fig. 4). The median saving was 8% (-29 to 36), and 20% of draws saved at least 23%. At November 2026 prices the system was cheaper in 98% of draws. The median draw costs more than the central case because the ranges for the net cost of electric cars, service growth, the discount rate and reactor cost extend further above their central values than below; the central case is therefore an optimistic point in the distribution. Cost was most sensitive to the net cost of battery-electric cars (Spearman ρ = 0.84), service growth (Spearman ρ = 0.41) and reactor capital cost (Spearman ρ = 0.24) (fig. S3). These shares describe the model's response to the assumed ranges, not the probability of an outcome, and they are conditional on one petroleum price; the break-even curve shows how they change with it (Fig. 4B).

The deterministic stress cases show which assumptions the result depends on (Table 2, fig. S5). The ownership cost of electric cars dominates. With no ownership advantage the saving falls to 14.7%; at USD 100 per year more than petrol cars it is 4.7%; and with the high derivation, which combines a dearer purchase, frequent battery replacement and a USD 300 insurance penalty, the system costs 36% more than petroleum. Removing carbon co-product revenue costs USD 26.5 billion (17.4% saving), and the optimizer then makes all hydrogen by steam electrolysis; commodity carbon markets alone give 18.6%. Combining USD 100 per year dearer electric cars with no carbon revenue makes the system 1.9% dearer than petroleum. Residue supply 25% and 50% lower gives savings of 16.4% and 5.8%, as the model replaces residues with more bamboo, hemp, energy crops and hub hydrogen. Limiting the fleet to 60 or 30 hubs costs little (22.2% and 21.5%), because pyrolysis, wind and, at 30 hubs, 219 GW of solar replace reactor heat and power; on an annual-cost basis reactor hubs lower cost but the result does not depend on them, although fewer hubs leave the system much less firm (section 3.2, wind-lull screen). Higher transmission and firming costs give 18.9%. The moderate combination costs USD 449 billion, 12% more than petroleum at late-2025 prices but 18% less at November 2026 prices; after gas it costs USD 469 billion. The severe combination costs USD 657 billion, above petroleum at either price.

The central solution needs 1,434 reactor modules of 250 MWt. Built from 2032 to 2050, that is about 75 modules, or 19 GW of heat, a year. China approved at least ten large reactors a year from 2022 to 2025 [51], about 12 GW of electric capacity, or roughly 30 GW of heat. In heat terms the hub programme would need about 63% of that rate, but about 8 times as many units a year as the roughly ten large reactors China now approves, so serial factory production of modules would have to be demonstrated first. The 322 GW of onshore wind needs about 13 GW a year until 2050, against 120 GW of wind added in China in 2025 [52], but it comes on top of wind built for other sectors. Firm supply from hubs and light-water reactors is 53% of electricity in the central case. In the wind-lull screen, the central system's hubs can deliver 133 GW when all their heat goes to the turbines, against an inflexible load of 189 GW; with residual wind the shortfall is 30 GW, or 5.1 TWh over the week (table S17). Covering it with hydrogen-fired backup would add about USD 3.1 billion a year (1.0% of system cost). Without nuclear power the shortfall is 97 GW and the indicative cost USD 10.0 billion; with at most 30 hubs it is 111 GW (USD 11.5 billion), so the small cost penalty of capping the hub fleet understates its effect on adequacy; after gas, with more hubs, it is 12 GW. The week's lost electrolytic hydrogen, 0.21 Mt, would have to come from storage. These numbers bound one stress period; they do not show that the system can follow load, hold reserves or stay stable hour by hour, which needs an hourly dispatch and network study.

### 3.3 Offshore wind and seaweed raise cost

Forcing half of each zone's large build-out, 230 GW of offshore wind with 22 Mt of dry seaweed, raises cost to USD 327.5 billion, a saving of 18.0% (median -1% across draws, cheaper in 48%). The full 460 GW build costs USD 357.7 billion (10.4% saving). Seaweed fuel costs USD 180 to 260 per MWh by zone. It is cheapest off Shandong, Liaoning and Fujian, where Saccharina yields are high, and dearest off Jiangsu, where nori yields are low (table S7, fig. S1). Seaweed does lower the marginal cost of liquids, which shows that land biomass is the binding resource. On cost grounds alone, though, offshore wind with seaweed does not pay. It would need benefits the model does not price, such as supply diversity, coastal nutrient removal in the eutrophic Yangtze and Pearl River plumes, or fishery co-production.

### 3.4 Natural gas for pyrolysis and hydrogen after gas

Pyrolysis runs only as far as its solid carbon finds buyers. The capped carbon markets take 17 Mt of carbon, which fixes pyrolysis at 5.8 Mt of hydrogen; beyond that, at China's gas price of USD 9.3 per MMBtu, steam electrolysis at the hubs is cheaper. Pyrolysis would then consume 33 bcm of natural gas a year by 2050, 8% of China's 2025 consumption (Fig. 6). With high reactor costs the optimizer uses more pyrolysis (16.5 Mt of hydrogen) and fewer hubs. In our gas balance, demand first exceeds domestic production, pipeline imports and a 140 bcm ceiling on liquefied gas imports in 2141 with the programme, against 2144 without it. Because China's resource base is uncertain, the date ranges from 2101 (40% recovery of remaining technically recoverable resources, or a smaller pool of pipeline gas) to 2175 (80% recovery). Building heat pumps and biogas extend it by a few years. The gas date is less binding than the import share: total imports reach 37% of supply.

In the gas era the system still burns fossil gas for pyrolysis and for part of pipeline compression. After gas it uses none: compressors are electric and steam electrolysis at the hubs makes all 16.6 Mt of hydrogen. The system then costs USD 329.7 billion with 283 hubs and 328 GW of wind, a saving of 17.4% (median 0% across draws, cheaper in 51%). Loss of pyrolysis carbon revenue explains most of the USD 26.3 billion increase. Emissions fall to 90 Mt (95.7%). With high steam-electrolysis electricity use (48 kWh per kg) the cost is USD 339.0 billion.

### 3.5 Reactor technologies and start-up fuel

The reactor comparison is a prospective technology sensitivity. The headline results use the HTR-PM, the only high-temperature module of the five in commercial operation; the other four rest on assumed costs, efficiencies and fuel-cycle characteristics for technologies at demonstration scale or earlier, and none of the conclusions above depends on them. Under those assumptions, the thorium molten-salt reactor has the lowest cost of the five technologies in both eras (Fig. 5A and table S4b): USD 287 billion in the gas era and USD 311 billion after gas. This reflects its assumed high turbine efficiency and low fuel-cycle cost, which are the least certain of our inputs, and it does not show that commercial thorium reactors would be China's cheapest option. The 950°C reactor follows (USD 300 and 326 billion), then the HTR-PM (303 and 330), the light-water small modular reactor (305 and 331) and the sodium fast reactor (313 and 345). The light-water reactor's low outlet temperature supplies little high-temperature heat, but its lower capital cost compensates. Among reactor parameters, capital cost matters most: low and high cost cases give USD 282.6 and 317.4 billion. At high cost the optimizer builds only 12 hubs and adds 228 GW of solar. Without any nuclear power, wind, solar and concentrated solar supply everything for USD 354.3 billion, a saving of 11.3%, with steam electrolysis heated by electric boilers and superheaters; with alkaline electrolysis only it is USD 361.3 billion (9.5%).

A speculative closed-cycle thorium molten-salt fleet, with no gas, is cheapest with 424 hubs at USD 306.0 billion (23.4% saving), and the same with nuclear power only. With 25% higher capital and USD 9 per MWh of heat for fuel recycling, the cost is USD 332.1 billion. The fleet needs 232 t of heavy metal a year. China's identified thorium of about 100,000 t [45] would last about 430 years at that rate, and a reported one million tonnes at Bayan Obo in Inner Mongolia about 4,300 years [53]. Start-up material is the constraint (Fig. 5B). A thermal-spectrum molten-salt fleet of 254 GWe would need about 380 t of fissile material. Supplied as high-assay low-enriched uranium, that would take about 82,000 t of natural uranium, a third of China's identified resource. A sodium fast fleet of 137 GWe would need about 1,370 t of plutonium, 1.2 times the plutonium projected in China's spent fuel by 2050 [47]. Military plutonium, estimated at 2.9 ± 0.6 t [48], could start about 0.3 GWe of fast reactors. Releasing it through arms reductions would be of little consequence for energy supply, whatever its other significance.

### 3.6 Autonomous regions, Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan

The regional results are screening allocations of the national solution by fixed shares, not a transmission-constrained regional optimisation. On that basis, all five autonomous regions supply more renewable liquids than their own 2050 demand (Fig. 7A). Inner Mongolia supplies 101 TWh from maize stover, sweet sorghum, Jerusalem artichoke and hemp, about 4.0 times its demand. Xinjiang supplies 83 TWh from cotton stalks, sweet sorghum and oilseed residues (3.8 times), and Guangxi 61 TWh from cassava, molasses, bagasse, jatropha and bamboo (1.7 times). Ningxia supplies 11 TWh and Xizang 4.2 TWh, 2.2 and 1.2 times their small demands. The four northern and western regions also hold most of China's wind, solar and concentrated solar potential, many hundreds of times their own transport electricity. Together the five regions cut well-to-wheel emissions from 189 to 10 Mt CO~2~e per year (Fig. 7B).

Hong Kong is dominated by aviation and bunker fuel. Of its 189 TWh of 2050 transport demand, 128 TWh are liquids that must come from the mainland. Macau is small and depends on Guangdong for electricity and fuel. Taiwan, as a separate island system, has little land biomass and imports 60 of its 69 TWh of liquids in the least-cost case at an assumed USD 170 per MWh. Its system costs USD 17.7 billion against USD 15.6 billion for petroleum, 13% more. Without nuclear power it costs USD 20.3 billion. Imported liquids account for USD 10 billion of Taiwan's cost; at about USD 140 per MWh instead of 170, Taiwan's system would match petroleum.

### 3.7 Food and cross-feeding

Low-grade heat from the least-cost hubs (289 TWh) could heat 75,000 ha of greenhouses producing 30.0 Mt of vegetables, and recirculating farms producing 3.0 Mt of fish and 2.0 Mt of shrimp. Drying heat could dry 80 Mt of grain and 0.5 Mt of food seaweed (Fig. 8A). The vegetables and seafood equal the food energy of about 11 million people, not complete diets. In the cross-feed balance (Fig. 8B), fishmeal from fish by-products, shrimp meal, insect meal grown on greenhouse waste, and Ulva grown on effluent nitrogen meet 51% of the marine protein needed in fish and shrimp feed and 23% of total feed protein, recover 78 kt of nitrogen, and displace 484 kt of imported fishmeal. Kelp and Ulva meet 44% of the algae needed for 0.3 Mt each of abalone and sea cucumber. Feed safety rules on recycled animal protein would need to be met. Asparagopsis grown with reactor heat could reduce enteric methane by up to 58 Mt CO~2~e per year, a scenario multiplier subject to dose–response uncertainty [54, 55]. None of these amounts enter the transport cost: heat sold to food production is given no value inside the transport boundary. Valued at USD 8 per MWh, before the cost of heat networks, exchangers and finding customers, the 193 TWh used would be worth about USD 1.5 billion a year to an integrated hub-and-food system.

### 3.8 Comparison with the United States

The China least-cost saving of 24.0% matches the 23.2% reported for the United States [11]. But the United States model's heavy-truck ratios divided kWh per mile by MJ per mile, understating heavy-truck electricity by a factor of 3.6. With corrected ratios and nothing else changed, the United States least-cost saving falls to 12.0%, and its post-gas saving from 16.4% to 4.7% (section S15). With the United States' uncorrected ratios, the China saving would be 38.3%. Measured consistently, the China system's saving is about twice that of the United States. China's advantages are cheaper wind, solar and reactors, a lower discount rate, cars that already cost no more electric than petrol, and larger residue supplies relative to liquid demand. These outweigh its lower pre-tax fuel prices and costlier natural gas. The United States code also allowed steam electrolysis only after gas. Lifting that restriction leaves the United States least-cost result unchanged, because cheaper gas there favours pyrolysis, but at China's gas price steam electrolysis becomes the main gas-era hydrogen route and lowers cost by USD 1.0 billion and gas use from 93 to 33 bcm a year (section S15).

## 4 Discussion

In the central case, China could replace petroleum in transport for about one-quarter less than petroleum at late-2025 prices, and for more than 40% less at November 2026 prices. This is a favourable modelled scenario, not a demonstrated or deliverable transition. The saving depends above all on electric cars costing no more to own than petrol cars. Average purchase prices in China already favour electric cars [21], but averages mix segments and features, insurance is dearer [24], and under the least favourable ownership-cost derivation the system costs 36% more than petroleum; it also depends, to a lesser degree, on carbon co-product markets that do not yet exist at this scale and on residue collection on a scale not yet attempted. Under a combination of moderately adverse assumptions the system costs more than petroleum at late-2025 prices, although less at November 2026 prices. In the conditional Monte Carlo analysis the median saving is 8%, the system is dearer than late-2025 petroleum in about 35% of draws, and at November 2026 prices it is cheaper in 98% of draws. Most of the saving comes from electrifying road transport, which China is already doing faster than other large economies [6–8]. The more novel elements play supporting roles. Reactor hubs supply firm electricity, process heat and hydrogen, from steam electrolysis and, while gas lasts and its carbon finds buyers, from pyrolysis. The autonomous regions' crops and China's crop residues supply the liquids that aviation, shipping and off-road machinery still need.

The binding resources are liquids and start-up fissile material, not electricity. Every land-biomass tier is used, and the marginal liquid costs about USD 210 per MWh. Better conversion yields, cheaper residue collection or lower liquid demand through further electrification of ships and machines would therefore lower cost most. Pyrolysis would add about 33 bcm a year to China's gas demand, and because it is limited by carbon markets rather than by gas, it brings the first gas shortfall forward by only 3 years. Import dependence matters more than the date: imports already approach 37% of supply without the programme. After gas, steam electrolysis keeps the system cheaper than petroleum. A closed thorium cycle would make the fuel supply effectively unlimited, but it is constrained by start-up fissile material and is not yet demonstrated beyond the 2 MWt TMSR-LF1, which reached a conversion ratio of about 0.1 [13]. Weapons plutonium is too small to matter: the energy case for a breeder fleet does not depend on it.

The regional results matter for policy because the autonomous regions are the main suppliers of low-cost renewable electricity and biomass, and several of them are among the lower-income parts of China. In the least-cost solution they export liquid fuels and power to the eastern provinces and the two special administrative regions. Hong Kong's aviation and bunker fuel demand, about 128 TWh, is among the largest single liquid-fuel loads in the system. Taiwan illustrates the limits of island systems. Electrification saves money, but with little land biomass, liquid fuels must be imported or made from offshore seaweed, and both cost more than petroleum at late-2025 prices. These are results of a cost model and carry no judgement on governance.

Several limits apply. The model balances energy annually and cannot show hourly adequacy, storage, ramping, transmission or system stability; the firm-share and solar-share constraints are surrogates, and the build-rate screen is not a deployment model. Construction rates, permitting, siting, biomass logistics and the qualification of pyrolysis carbon for battery and fibre markets are not modelled. Soil-carbon losses from residue removal, which could be large, are treated only as a life-cycle boundary sensitivity, and the Monte Carlo draws are independent, so correlated adverse outcomes are represented only by the stress cases. Biomass availability, liquid-fuel yields at national scale, jet-fuel certification and reactor costs and learning are uncertain, and the reactor-type comparison rests on assumed costs and efficiencies for technologies that are at demonstration scale. Regional results split a national optimization by fixed shares and are not a regional optimization. Many baseline splits, for example of diesel use between segments and of Hong Kong and Taiwan fuel by use, are assumptions (tables S1 and S8), and petroleum prices are historical snapshots, not forecasts. The schematic map is not a siting study.

## 5 Conclusions

An open annual model of a petroleum-free transport system for mainland China, its five autonomous regions, Hong Kong and Macau finds a central least-cost 2050 system costing USD 303.4 billion per year, 24.0% less than petroleum at late-2025 pre-tax prices, with a cut of 94.8% in well-to-wheel emissions (85% with the widest life-cycle boundary tested). The result is conditional. It holds if battery-electric cars cost no more to own than petrol cars; without that advantage the saving falls to 14.7%, and moderately adverse assumptions taken together make the system dearer than petroleum at late-2025 prices but not at November 2026 prices. Reactor hubs lower cost and, more importantly, firm the system: with at most 30 hubs the saving is still 21.5% on an annual basis, but the wind-lull screen then indicates about USD 11.5 billion a year of backup that the optimisation does not include. Steam electrolysis at the hubs supplies most hydrogen; pyrolysis, limited by markets for its carbon, needs about 33 bcm of gas a year, and after gas the central system remains 17.4% cheaper than petroleum. The reactor-type comparison is prospective: a thorium molten-salt reactor has the lowest cost under assumed inputs, but start-up fissile material, not thorium or uranium resources, limits a breeder fleet, and plutonium from weapons stocks is negligible for this purpose. The autonomous regions are net suppliers of renewable fuel; Taiwan's island system costs more than petroleum unless imported renewable liquids become cheaper. Corrected for a unit error in heavy-truck energy, the related United States model gives about half China's saving. The results establish annual resource adequacy, not operational feasibility. Hourly dispatch, reserves and transmission, deployment trajectories, residue sustainability at field scale, carbon-product qualification and demonstration of biomass conversion, seaweed harvesting and advanced reactors at scale are the next steps.
